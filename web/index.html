Disc Golf Flight Lab

A disc in flight is a spinning, bevelled wing. It generates lift, it resists the air, and because it is a gyroscope, the torque from that airflow does not tip it over — it turns it sideways. This simulates that behaviour directly: a fixed‑step RK4 integrator runs at 240 Hz over lift, drag and pitching‑moment tables that come, for four discs, from published CFD. The same model drives both modes below.

How the flight model works

Each throw is integrated forward in small time steps. At every step the simulation asks the same four questions:

  1. Orientation vs. airflow

    The disc's angle of attack is the angle between where it is pointing and where it is actually going — which drift apart as the throw progresses. This angle drives everything else.

  2. Lift and drag

    Angle of attack maps to lift and drag coefficients, scaled by air density and the square of airspeed. Lift is what makes a well‑thrown disc float far past where a ballistic arc would have dropped it.

  3. Pitching moment and precession

    The aerodynamic centre sits ahead of the centre of mass, so airflow applies a torque. On a spinning disc that torque does not cause a tip — it causes precession 90° out of phase. This is the real source of turn and fade.

  4. Spin decay

    Spin bleeds off over the flight — 8–12% on a full drive. As it does, the disc resists precession less, which is why fade arrives late and hard rather than evenly across the throw.

Integrated with fixed‑step RK4 at 1/240 s. The disc is axisymmetric, so the spin phase never enters the equations of motion — only the spin rate does. Leaving it out removes the only stiff timescale in the system, which is what makes 240 Hz enough.

What this is, and what it is not

A simulator that states its limits precisely is more useful than one that does not. The same text is in the app, behind the ? button.

  1. Four discs are measured. Ten are derived.

    Real aerodynamic data exists for exactly four disc golf discs: peer‑reviewed CFD on 3D scans of real moulds (Giljarhus, Kristiansen, Tutkun & Oggiano, Sports Engineering 25:24, 2022). The other ten discs in the roster are derived — their coefficients are constructed by mapping geometry and published flight numbers onto those four anchors, through a regression with n = 4 (R² 0.89 and 0.91). An R² on four points is barely constrained. Every disc carries its provenance and the interface shows it everywhere the disc appears.

  2. The precession gain is a fudge factor

    Turn and fade come from torque precessing a gyroscope. Those kinematics are exact and proven in CI: the rate is M / (I_zz · spin). On top of that the model applies PRECESSION_GAIN = 2.0, which is empirical, not derived. It stands in for spin‑dependent aerodynamics the source data structurally cannot contain — the CFD is steady‑state on a non‑rotating disc, so it has no spin‑induced rolling moment. It is a fudge factor with a plausible story, not a measurement.

  3. Known defect: it over-turns at low hyzer

    Because that one gain scales the whole precession response, it doubles the early turn as well as the late fade. Every roster disc rated turn −1 or lower turns over and finishes right from a flat release — including a 12‑speed Destroyer, which in reality finishes left. Discs rated turn 0 behave correctly. The release defaults compensate, which is why a distance driver starts at 22° of hyzer: more than a real thrower uses. Recorded rather than hidden.

  4. Glide is not fittable, and damping is inert

    Three of the four measured discs are rated glide 5 and one glide 3 — one contrast, buried inside the scatter among the glide‑5 discs themselves. Glide ships flagged as an author‑chosen heuristic rather than a fit. Pitch and roll damping are Hummel's 2003 numbers for an Ultimate disc, used unchanged because no disc‑golf measurement exists; under this model's scaling they contribute about 0.1% of the static pitching moment, so they do essentially nothing.

  5. 60 fps is unverified

    There is no GPU on the machine this was built and tested on: llvmpipe gives 5–7 fps and SwiftShader 1–2, both pure CPU rasterisation, and neither says anything about real hardware. The simulation costs ~0.048 ms of CPU per frame and the scene draws ~190 calls in Flight Lab and 135–183 in Portal Puzzles with portals rendering, which is trivial for any integrated GPU of the last decade — so smooth playback is a reasonable expectation and not a measurement.

  6. A wall portal is free. A dive portal is not, and that is real

    The equations are equivariant under rotations about world‑up and only those, because gravity is the only term that does not rotate with the disc. So a wall‑to‑wall portal pair — pure yaw — reproduces the un‑portalled flight exactly, measured to 1.9×10−6 m, which is the float32 precision of the transform itself. Nothing changes but which room's wind you are in. Turn the portal upside down and lift points down: an inverted disc is an inverted wing. Measured across the roster, a dive portal costs 40–45% of the distance and collapses the flight to 2.5–2.9 s whatever you throw. That is correct physics used deliberately as a mechanic, not a scripted effect — and it is why those levels are signposted in orange everywhere they appear.

  7. The ten solutions are asserted in CI

    Every level ships with the throw that solves it and the distance that throw actually lands from the flag — a measurement taken against the shipped integrator, not an estimate. CI replays all ten through the real game logic and fails the build if any lands more than 0.5 m from its recorded result. It is a physics regression test wearing level content as its fixture: change a coefficient, the precession gain or the portal transform, and it says which level broke and by how many metres. Without it a level could quietly become unsolvable.
